# Scene context

Read this before breaking a scene into shots, or when a scene's shots feel
generic — technically correct framing that could belong to any film.

A scene is not just an action line. Before covering it, a real production has
six people looking at the same page and seeing different things. Work through
all six yourself. It takes one pass and it is the difference between "medium
shot of two people talking" and a shot that carries the scene.

Do not treat these as six separate jobs to hand off. It is one reading from six
angles, and the value is in how they contradict each other — the gaffer wants
the practical lamp motivated, the colorist wants the shadows crushed, and
resolving that tension is the actual decision.

---

## Gaffer — light

What is the key, where is it coming from, and is it motivated by something in
frame? Practicals matter: a streetlamp, a laptop, a match. If the light has no
source in the world, the image reads as rendered rather than photographed.

Ask what shifts during the scene. Light that changes — a door opening, a car
passing, a cigarette flaring — gives an editor something to cut on and gives the
scene a clock.

Check the scene's `time` field and let it govern everything here. A night
interior lit like golden hour is the single most common failure, and it survives
because every individual shot prompt reads plausibly on its own.

## VFX — air

Not explosions. The small environmental things that make a frame feel inhabited:
dust in a shaft of light, breath in cold air, steam, rain on glass, heat
shimmer, smoke, lens flare from a practical.

A room with clean empty air looks like a render. One atmospheric element per
shot is usually enough, and it should be consistent across the scene — dust
motes in shot 2 and pristine air in shot 5 is a continuity break nobody can
name but everybody feels.

## Hair & makeup — state

Not what a character looks like in general — the bible already holds that. What
they look like *now*, in this scene, after what has just happened to them.

Sweating, crying, bleeding, immaculate, disheveled, drunk, exhausted, freshly
showered. This is where a scene's place in the story becomes visible on a face.
If a character was running in the previous scene and arrives composed in this
one, the cut will read as a mistake.

## Key grip — camera energy

Locked-off, handheld, or smooth? This is a decision about control.

Static framing gives the audience nowhere to look away — it suits menace,
formality, and dread. Handheld puts the viewer inside a body and suits panic and
intimacy. Smooth movement suits elegance, wealth, and inevitability.

Pick one energy for the scene and hold it. Mixing them without reason makes the
coverage feel assembled rather than directed.

## Colorist — grade

The project moodboard sets the palette. Ask what this specific scene does within
it: is it the warmest scene in a cold film, the one moment of saturation, the
point where the grade starts to drain?

A grade that never varies across a film is a look. A grade that shifts with the
story is cinematography.

**The moodboard is a grade and an atmosphere, not a set dressing list.** If the
moodboard says "neon reflections, wet asphalt," that does not mean every scene
gains neon signs and rain. It means the colour separation and the quality of
light. Applying moodboard keywords as objects is the most common way a scene
ends up with props nobody asked for.

## Subtext — what is unsaid

What is actually happening under the dialogue? Who has power at the start of the
scene, who has it at the end, and where does it change hands?

This is what tells you where to put the camera. If a character is losing
control, the coverage can tighten on them while their opposite is given air. If
someone is lying, the camera can stay on the listener. Subtext is the reason to
prefer one shot over another when both are technically fine.

---

## Using this

Write the six down before listing shots. Keep them short — a line or two each.
They are working notes, not a document.

Then, when you write each shot, the light, the air, the state, the energy, and
the grade all have answers already. That is what `ambient_scene` is for, and why
a shot that skips this reads as a description of an event rather than a frame.

If the scene has set design, wardrobe, or a location with spatial notes, read
those first — they constrain the gaffer and the colorist answers, and it is
cheaper to read them than to write a shot list that contradicts the set.
